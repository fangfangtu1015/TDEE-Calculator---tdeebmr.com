Understanding Ideal Body Weight

Ideal Body Weight (IBW) is a clinical estimate of the healthiest weight for a given height. It was originally developed for use in medical settings, particularly for calculating drug dosages, assessing nutritional status, and determining healthy weight ranges. Today, IBW formulas are used by healthcare providers, dietitians, and fitness professionals as one of several tools for evaluating body composition.

It is important to understand that "ideal" weight is not a single number but rather a range that depends on your body composition, frame size, age, sex, and health goals. The formulas below provide statistical estimates based on population data, not personalized prescriptions.

The Four Clinical Formulas

Devine Formula (1974)

Developed by Dr. B.J. Devine for calculating drug dosages, the Devine formula is the most widely used IBW equation in clinical medicine. It was derived from a study of height-weight relationships in a working population.

Devine Formula

Male: 50.0 kg + 2.3 kg per inch over 5 feet
Female: 45.5 kg + 2.3 kg per inch over 5 feet

The Devine formula is used in hospital settings for calculating tidal volume in mechanical ventilation, drug dosages (particularly for medications that distribute primarily in lean tissue), and nutritional assessment. It tends to produce values in the middle of the range compared to other formulas.

Robinson Formula (1983)

Dr. J.D. Robinson revised the Devine formula in 1983 to better reflect contemporary body compositions. The Robinson formula generally produces slightly lower values than Devine, particularly for taller individuals.

Robinson Formula

Male: 52.0 kg + 1.9 kg per inch over 5 feet
Female: 49.0 kg + 1.7 kg per inch over 5 feet

The Robinson formula was developed by analyzing data from a broader population sample and is sometimes preferred in research settings where a more conservative IBW estimate is desired.

Miller Formula (1983)

Dr. D.R. Miller published his formula in the same year as Robinson, but with higher base weights and per-inch additions. The Miller formula produces the highest IBW estimates among the four and may be more appropriate for larger-framed individuals.

Miller Formula

Male: 56.2 kg + 1.41 kg per inch over 5 feet
Female: 53.1 kg + 1.36 kg per inch over 5 feet

The Miller formula was derived from life insurance data and aims to represent the weight associated with the lowest mortality risk. It tends to give values that correspond to a BMI of approximately 24-26.

Hamwi Formula (1964)

The Hamwi formula is the oldest of the four, developed by Dr. G.J. Hamwi in 1964 for clinical nutrition assessment. Despite its age, it remains widely used in dietetic practice.

Hamwi Formula

Male: 48.0 kg + 2.7 kg per inch over 5 feet
Female: 45.5 kg + 2.2 kg per inch over 5 feet

The Hamwi formula uses the highest per-inch increment for males, making it produce relatively high estimates for tall men. It was originally designed as a quick bedside tool for dietitians to assess whether patients were at a healthy weight.

Healthy BMI Range

In addition to the four IBW formulas, the healthy BMI range provides a weight interval based on the WHO's healthy BMI classification (18.5-24.9 kg/m2). For your height, this translates to:

BMI Range Calculation

Lower bound: 18.5 x height(m)2
Upper bound: 24.9 x height(m)2

The BMI range is broader than most IBW formulas because it includes the full spectrum of weights considered healthy by WHO standards. IBW formulas typically correspond to a BMI of 22-26, which is near the middle or slightly above the center of the healthy range.

Limitations of Ideal Weight Formulas

All IBW formulas share a fundamental limitation: they use only height and sex as inputs, ignoring body composition, frame size, age, and ethnicity. This means:

  • Muscular individuals may have an IBW that is significantly below their actual healthy weight. A competitive male bodybuilder at 180 cm might weigh 95 kg with 10% body fat, well above any IBW formula's estimate, yet be extremely healthy.
  • Elderly individuals may benefit from slightly higher weights than IBW suggests, as some additional body fat is protective against frailty and osteoporosis.
  • Ethnic differences in body composition mean that a single formula cannot be equally accurate for all populations. Asian populations, for example, experience elevated health risks at lower BMIs.
  • Frame size (small, medium, large) can account for 10% variation in healthy weight at the same height, but no IBW formula accounts for this.

Key Takeaway

Ideal body weight formulas are useful reference points but should never be the sole determinant of your health or weight goals. Use the average of the four formulas as a starting point, then factor in your body composition (body fat percentage), waist circumference, fitness level, and how you feel. For a more personalized assessment, combine IBW with BMI, body fat percentage, and lean body mass calculations.
