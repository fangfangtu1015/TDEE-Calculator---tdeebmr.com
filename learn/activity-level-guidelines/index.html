Activity Level Guidelines: 5 PAL Levels Explained

November 4, 2026 • 11 min read Fundamentals Training

The single biggest source of error in TDEE calculation is not the BMR equation itself; it is the activity multiplier. The difference between choosing "lightly active" (1.375) and "moderately active" (1.55) can be 300+ calories per day for the average adult. Over a week, that is 2,100 calories, or roughly 0.6 lbs of fat. Choose wrong, and your diet plan starts from a flawed foundation.

This guide provides detailed, practical descriptions of each Physical Activity Level (PAL) multiplier, explains what actually counts as exercise, addresses how occupation affects your total activity, and gives you a framework for making the right choice even when your activity varies from week to week.

What Is a PAL Multiplier?

PAL stands for Physical Activity Level. It is a ratio that represents your total daily energy expenditure (TDEE) divided by your basal metabolic rate (BMR). In simple terms, it answers the question: "How many times my resting metabolism do I actually burn in a day?"

A PAL of 1.2 means you burn 20% more calories than your BMR (typical for someone who sits all day). A PAL of 1.9 means you burn 90% more (typical for elite athletes or heavy manual laborers).

As explained in our TDEE vs. BMR article, the gap between BMR and TDEE is filled by three components: the thermic effect of food (TEF, roughly 10%), exercise activity thermogenesis (EAT, roughly 5-10%), and non-exercise activity thermogenesis (NEAT, roughly 6-50%). The PAL multiplier is a rough way of capturing all three in a single number.

The Five Activity Levels in Detail

Level 1: Sedentary (PAL 1.2)

Who this describes:

  • Office/desk job with no physically demanding tasks
  • No structured exercise or very occasional light activity (a walk once a week)
  • Minimal movement throughout the day: drive to work, sit at desk, drive home, sit on couch
  • Step count: typically under 5,000 steps per day

Real-world example: A software developer who works from home, orders delivery for lunch, and spends evenings watching TV. Gets no regular exercise. May walk 3,000-4,000 steps per day.

Common misclassification: People who exercise once per week for 30 minutes often think they should be "lightly active." If the other 6 days of the week are truly sedentary, 1.2 is the correct choice. One 30-minute workout adds approximately 200-300 calories to a weekly total of roughly 15,000-17,000. That is a rounding error, not a meaningful shift in activity level.

Level 2: Lightly Active (PAL 1.375)

Who this describes:

  • Light exercise or sports 1-3 days per week (30-45 minutes per session)
  • OR a job that requires significant standing or walking (teacher, retail worker, restaurant server) but no heavy lifting
  • Step count: typically 5,000-7,500 steps per day

Real-world example: An office worker who goes to the gym for 30-45 minutes three times per week (mix of weights and light cardio), walks the dog daily, and takes the stairs instead of the elevator. Or a retail employee who is on their feet for 6-8 hour shifts but does no additional exercise.

Common misclassification: People who do 30 minutes of light activity most days but consider themselves "moderately active." The key word is light. If your exercise does not significantly elevate your heart rate or cause sweating, it falls into this category regardless of duration.

Level 3: Moderately Active (PAL 1.55)

Who this describes:

  • Moderate-intensity exercise or sports 3-5 days per week (45-60 minutes per session)
  • OR a physically demanding job (construction worker, nurse, warehouse worker, mail carrier) plus some additional exercise
  • Step count: typically 7,500-10,000 steps per day

Real-world example: Someone who does strength training or moderate-intensity cardio (jogging, cycling, swimming) for 45-60 minutes, 4 times per week. They have a desk job but commute by bike or walk to the gym. Or a nurse who works 12-hour shifts on their feet, walking 8+ miles per shift, and does light exercise on days off.

Common misclassification: This is the level most active gym-goers should select. Many people who train 4-5 times per week choose "very active" (1.725) because they think their gym sessions are more intense than they actually are. If you have a desk job and train for 45-60 minutes 4 times per week, you are moderately active, not very active.

The "When in Doubt, Go Lower" Principle

Research consistently shows that people overestimate their activity level. A 2019 study in the Journal of Nutrition found that when participants self-selected their PAL category, 68% chose a level higher than what accelerometer data supported. The practical consequence: choosing a lower activity level and adding calories later if needed is almost always better than starting too high and wondering why you are not losing weight.

Level 4: Very Active (PAL 1.725)

Who this describes:

  • Hard exercise or sports 6-7 days per week (60+ minutes per session)
  • OR a very physically demanding job (construction, farming, firefighting) combined with regular exercise
  • Step count: typically 10,000-15,000 steps per day

Real-world example: A competitive amateur athlete who trains intensely 6 days per week (running, cycling, swimming, or team sports) for 60-90 minutes per session. Or a construction worker who performs heavy physical labor for 8-10 hours per day and also goes to the gym 3 times per week.

Common misclassification: Weekend warriors who train very hard on Saturday and Sunday but are sedentary during the week should not use this level. "Very active" requires consistent daily activity, not two intense sessions per week.

Level 5: Extra Active (PAL 1.9)

Who this describes:

  • Professional or elite amateur athletes in heavy training phases
  • Manual laborers (e.g., agricultural workers, movers) who perform intense physical work 8+ hours per day, 6-7 days per week
  • Individuals who train twice per day with high intensity
  • Step count: typically over 15,000 steps per day

Real-world example: An Olympic-level endurance athlete training 4-6 hours per day. A Tour de France cyclist during race season (burning 6,000-8,000+ kcal/day). A farmer performing heavy manual labor from dawn to dusk, 6 days per week.

Common misclassification: Almost no one should use this level unless they are a competitive athlete or heavy manual laborer. If you think you are "extra active" because you go to the gym every day and walk a lot, you are probably "very active" at most. The 1.9 multiplier is reserved for truly exceptional levels of physical output sustained over many hours per day.

What Actually Counts as Exercise?

Not all physical activity is created equal. Here is how different activities contribute to your PAL:

Activity Calories Burned (per 30 min, 170 lb person) Contribution to PAL
Sitting at desk / working on computer ~35-40 kcal Minimal (already counted in BMR)
Standing (casual) ~50-60 kcal Small increase over sitting
Walking (3 mph, casual pace) ~120-140 kcal Moderate contribution
Weight training (moderate intensity) ~130-170 kcal Moderate contribution; afterburn effect adds 5-15%
Running (6 mph / 10 min mile) ~280-340 kcal Significant contribution
Cycling (moderate, 12-14 mph) ~240-280 kcal Significant contribution
HIIT / vigorous interval training ~250-350 kcal High contribution; significant afterburn effect
Swimming (moderate effort) ~220-280 kcal High contribution

The critical insight: a 45-minute gym session burns approximately 200-350 calories. If you then sit for the remaining 15 hours of your waking day, that session contributes only a small fraction to your total daily expenditure. This is why occupation and daily movement patterns matter more than gym sessions alone.

How to Handle Variable Activity Weeks

Few people have perfectly consistent activity levels. Here is how to handle common variability scenarios:

Scenario: Active Weekday, Sedentary Weekend

If you have a physically demanding job Monday through Friday but sit on the couch all weekend, your weekly average activity is lower than your weekday activity suggests. Calculate your TDEE using the activity level that represents your 7-day average, not your most active day.

Approach: Estimate the total calories burned across the week. If you burn 3,000 kcal/day on weekdays and 2,000 kcal/day on weekends, your weekly average is approximately 2,700 kcal/day. Use this average to determine your effective PAL.

Scenario: Inconsistent Exercise Schedule

If some weeks you exercise 5 times and other weeks you exercise once, use your monthly average. Most people find that their typical week involves 2-4 exercise sessions, which corresponds to "lightly active" to "moderately active."

Scenario: Seasonal Variation

If you are much more active in summer (outdoor sports, swimming, hiking) than in winter, recalculate your TDEE seasonally. Use a higher PAL in summer and a lower one in winter. Alternatively, use a single annual average if you prefer simplicity.

Step Count Correlations

For those who track steps with a pedometer or smartwatch, here are rough correlations between daily step count and PAL categories:

Daily Step Count Approximate PAL Category Notes
Under 5,000 steps/day Sedentary (1.2) Typical office worker with no exercise routine
5,000-7,500 steps/day Lightly Active (1.375) Some daily movement plus light exercise
7,500-10,000 steps/day Moderately Active (1.55) Active lifestyle or regular exercise routine
10,000-15,000 steps/day Very Active (1.725) Physically demanding job or serious training
Over 15,000 steps/day Extra Active (1.9) Elite athlete or heavy manual laborer

Important Caveat

Step counts are only one component of activity. A person who does 45 minutes of intense cycling may burn as many calories as someone who walks 10,000 steps, even though the cyclist's step count is low. Use step counts as a rough guideline, not a precise determinant. Also, step counts do not capture the intensity of movement; 5,000 steps of brisk walking burns significantly more than 5,000 steps of slow shuffling.

The Job Activity Impact

Your occupation is one of the largest determinants of daily energy expenditure. Consider these estimates for common jobs:

Occupation Physical Demand Estimated Daily Work Calories (8-hr shift) Recommended PAL Starting Point
Software developer / office worker Sedentary ~1,200-1,400 kcal 1.2 (no exercise) to 1.375 (with exercise)
Teacher / retail worker Light ~1,500-1,800 kcal 1.375
Nurse / warehouse worker Moderate ~1,800-2,200 kcal 1.55
Construction worker / farmer Heavy ~2,200-2,800 kcal 1.725
Competitive athlete / heavy laborer Extreme ~2,800-4,000+ kcal 1.9

These are rough estimates. Individual variation is significant based on body size, efficiency of movement, and environmental conditions. Use your occupation as a starting point for choosing your PAL, then validate with empirical tracking.

Practical Decision Framework

When choosing your activity level, work through this decision tree:

  1. What is your occupation? If it requires heavy physical labor for 8+ hours, start at PAL 1.55 or higher. If it is primarily desk work, start at 1.2-1.375.
  2. How many days per week do you exercise? Add one level for 1-3 days of moderate exercise, two levels for 4-6 days. But do not go above 1.725 unless you are a competitive athlete.
  3. What is your average daily step count? Use the step count table above as a cross-check.
  4. When in doubt, go lower. It is easier to add calories than to figure out why you are not losing weight.
  5. Validate empirically. Track food and weight for 2-3 weeks. If results match expectations, your PAL is correct. If not, adjust.

The Bottom Line

Your activity level is the most impactful and most commonly misestimated variable in TDEE calculation. The BMR equation is reasonably accurate for most people; the PAL multiplier is where errors creep in. Choose conservatively, validate with real-world data, and adjust as needed.
