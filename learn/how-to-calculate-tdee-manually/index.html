How to Calculate TDEE Manually: A Step-by-Step Guide

November 10, 2026 • 11 min read Fundamentals Science

Online calculators are convenient, but understanding how TDEE is calculated empowers you to make informed adjustments, compare formulas, and troubleshoot when results do not match expectations. This guide walks you through the entire manual calculation process using the two most respected equations in nutrition science: Mifflin-St Jeor and Katch-McArdle.

By the end, you will be able to calculate your own TDEE on paper, understand the assumptions behind each formula, and know when to switch from one to the other.

The Two-Step Process

Calculating TDEE manually always involves two steps:

  1. Calculate BMR (Basal Metabolic Rate) using a prediction equation
  2. Multiply BMR by an activity factor (PAL multiplier) to get TDEE

As explained in our TDEE vs. BMR article, BMR represents your resting energy needs (about 60-75% of total expenditure), while TDEE adds the calories burned through digestion, movement, and exercise.

Step 1: Calculate BMR Using Mifflin-St Jeor

The Mifflin-St Jeor equation, published in 1990, is the most widely recommended formula for estimating BMR in the general population. The American Dietetic Association endorsed it as the most accurate prediction equation for non-obese and obese individuals alike (see our comparison of BMR formulas for how it stacks up against alternatives).

The Equations

For men:

BMR = (10 × weight in kg) + (6.25 × height in cm) - (5 × age in years) + 5

For women:

BMR = (10 × weight in kg) + (6.25 × height in cm) - (5 × age in years) - 161

Unit Conversions

If you measure in imperial units, convert first:

  • Weight: pounds ÷ 2.205 = kilograms
  • Height: inches × 2.54 = centimeters

Worked Example: Male, 30 years old, 180 lbs, 5'10"

Convert units:

  • Weight: 180 ÷ 2.205 = 81.6 kg
  • Height: 70 inches × 2.54 = 177.8 cm

Apply formula:

  • BMR = (10 × 81.6) + (6.25 × 177.8) - (5 × 30) + 5
  • BMR = 816 + 1,111.25 - 150 + 5
  • BMR = 1,782 kcal/day

Worked Example: Female, 28 years old, 145 lbs, 5'5"

Convert units:

  • Weight: 145 ÷ 2.205 = 65.8 kg
  • Height: 65 inches × 2.54 = 165.1 cm

Apply formula:

  • BMR = (10 × 65.8) + (6.25 × 165.1) - (5 × 28) - 161
  • BMR = 658 + 1,031.9 - 140 - 161
  • BMR = 1,389 kcal/day

Step 2: Multiply by Activity Factor to Get TDEE

Once you have BMR, multiply it by the appropriate Physical Activity Level (PAL) multiplier:

Activity Level PAL Multiplier Description
Sedentary 1.2 Desk job, little or no structured exercise, minimal movement throughout the day
Lightly Active 1.375 Light exercise or sports 1-3 days/week, or a job that requires standing/walking most of the day
Moderately Active 1.55 Moderate exercise or sports 3-5 days/week, or a physically demanding job (construction, nursing)
Very Active 1.725 Hard exercise or sports 6-7 days/week, or a very physically demanding job plus daily exercise
Extra Active 1.9 Professional athlete, manual laborer training twice daily, or extremely active lifestyle

Continuing the Examples

Male (BMR = 1,782), moderately active (PAL = 1.55):

TDEE = 1,782 × 1.55 = 2,762 kcal/day

Female (BMR = 1,389), lightly active (PAL = 1.375):

TDEE = 1,389 × 1.375 = 1,910 kcal/day

Choosing the Right Activity Level

The activity multiplier is where most calculation errors occur. People consistently overestimate their activity level, which inflates TDEE and leads to overeating. When in doubt, choose the lower level. You can always increase calories later if weight loss stalls. See our activity level guidelines for detailed descriptions of what each level actually looks like in daily life.

Alternative: Katch-McArdle Formula (When You Know Body Fat %)

If you know your body fat percentage (from DEXA scan, hydrostatic weighing, or reliable skinfold measurements), the Katch-McArdle formula is more accurate than Mifflin-St Jeor, especially for muscular or overweight individuals.

The Equation

BMR = 370 + (21.6 × lean body mass in kg)

Where lean body mass = total body weight × (1 - body fat percentage / 100)

Worked Example: Male, 180 lbs, 15% body fat

Calculate lean body mass:

  • Total weight: 180 lbs = 81.6 kg
  • Fat mass: 81.6 × 0.15 = 12.24 kg
  • Lean body mass: 81.6 - 12.24 = 69.36 kg

Apply Katch-McArdle:

  • BMR = 370 + (21.6 × 69.36)
  • BMR = 370 + 1,498
  • BMR = 1,868 kcal/day

Compare with Mifflin-St Jeor for the same person:

  • Mifflin-St Jeor BMR (from earlier): 1,782 kcal/day
  • Katch-McArdle BMR: 1,868 kcal/day
  • Difference: +86 kcal/day (Katch-McArdle is higher because this person is muscular with low body fat)

Whether Katch-McArdle lands above or below Mifflin-St Jeor depends on how lean the person actually is, not simply on whether they carry extra fat. Mifflin-St Jeor adds a fixed amount per kilogram of total body weight, so it keeps crediting fat tissue with the same metabolic activity as lean tissue, while Katch-McArdle discounts fat tissue entirely. That means the gap shrinks as body fat rises, but the two usually do not cross over until somewhere above 35-40% body fat: at the 15% body fat in the example above, Katch-McArdle is 86 kcal higher, and at the 32% body fat in Scenario 1 below it is still 150 kcal higher.

When to Use Each Formula

Mifflin-St Jeor: Best for the general population when you do not know body fat percentage. Most accurate for people with average body composition.
Katch-McArdle: Best when you know your body fat percentage. More accurate for muscular individuals (underestimates their BMR less) and those with obesity (overestimates their BMR less). See our full formula comparison for detailed accuracy data.

Worked Examples for Different Scenarios

Scenario 1: Overweight Female Starting a Cut

Profile: Female, 42 years old, 200 lbs, 5'4", body fat approximately 32%, sedentary to lightly active.

Using Mifflin-St Jeor:

  • Weight: 200 ÷ 2.205 = 90.7 kg
  • Height: 64 × 2.54 = 162.6 cm
  • BMR = (10 × 90.7) + (6.25 × 162.6) - (5 × 42) - 161
  • BMR = 907 + 1,016.25 - 210 - 161 = 1,552 kcal/day
  • TDEE (sedentary, 1.2) = 1,552 × 1.2 = 1,862 kcal/day
  • TDEE (lightly active, 1.375) = 1,552 × 1.375 = 2,134 kcal/day

Using Katch-McArdle (32% body fat):

  • Lean body mass: 90.7 × (1 - 0.32) = 61.7 kg
  • BMR = 370 + (21.6 × 61.7) = 370 + 1,333 = 1,703 kcal/day
  • TDEE (sedentary, 1.2) = 1,703 × 1.2 = 2,044 kcal/day

Notice the significant difference: Mifflin-St Jeor gives a BMR of 1,552 while Katch-McArdle gives 1,703. For this individual, Katch-McArdle is likely more accurate because it accounts for her actual lean body mass. However, both are estimates. The true test is empirical validation: track intake and weight for 2-3 weeks.

Scenario 2: Athletic Male Bulking

Profile: Male, 25 years old, 170 lbs, 5'11", body fat approximately 10%, very active (trains 6 days/week).

Using Mifflin-St Jeor:

  • Weight: 170 ÷ 2.205 = 77.1 kg
  • Height: 71 × 2.54 = 180.3 cm
  • BMR = (10 × 77.1) + (6.25 × 180.3) - (5 × 25) + 5
  • BMR = 771 + 1,126.9 - 125 + 5 = 1,778 kcal/day
  • TDEE (very active, 1.725) = 1,778 × 1.725 = 3,067 kcal/day
  • Bulk target (TDEE + 300) = 3,367 kcal/day

Using Katch-McArdle (10% body fat):

  • Lean body mass: 77.1 × (1 - 0.10) = 69.4 kg
  • BMR = 370 + (21.6 × 69.4) = 370 + 1,499 = 1,869 kcal/day
  • TDEE (very active, 1.725) = 1,869 × 1.725 = 3,224 kcal/day
  • Bulk target (TDEE + 300) = 3,524 kcal/day

For this very lean, muscular athlete, Katch-McArdle gives a BMR that is 91 kcal higher than Mifflin-St Jeor. This makes sense: his high muscle mass means his actual resting metabolism is higher than what total-body-weight-based equations predict. For a lean bulk, the difference between the two formulas translates to approximately 157 kcal at the final target.

Scenario 3: Older Sedentary Male

Profile: Male, 58 years old, 220 lbs, 5'8", body fat approximately 28%, sedentary.

Using Mifflin-St Jeor:

  • Weight: 220 ÷ 2.205 = 99.8 kg
  • Height: 68 × 2.54 = 172.7 cm
  • BMR = (10 × 99.8) + (6.25 × 172.7) - (5 × 58) + 5
  • BMR = 998 + 1,079.4 - 290 + 5 = 1,792 kcal/day
  • TDEE (sedentary, 1.2) = 1,792 × 1.2 = 2,150 kcal/day

Using Katch-McArdle (28% body fat):

  • Lean body mass: 99.8 × (1 - 0.28) = 71.9 kg
  • BMR = 370 + (21.6 × 71.9) = 370 + 1,553 = 1,923 kcal/day
  • TDEE (sedentary, 1.2) = 1,923 × 1.2 = 2,308 kcal/day

Interestingly, Katch-McArdle gives a higher BMR here despite the man being overweight. This is because his lean body mass (71.9 kg) is still substantial, and the formula rewards that. Mifflin-St Jeor uses total body weight, which includes fat mass that is less metabolically active. In this case, Katch-McArdle is likely more accurate, but the true test is always empirical tracking.

Adjusting Based on Results

Regardless of which formula you use, the calculation is only a starting point. Here is how to refine your estimate:

The Empirical Validation Protocol

  1. Track food intake using a digital kitchen scale for 14-21 days.
  2. Weigh yourself daily (morning, after bathroom, before eating) and calculate weekly averages.
  3. Compare average intake to average weight change:
    • If weight is stable: your average intake = your actual TDEE
    • If losing 0.5 lbs/week: your actual TDEE = average intake + 250 kcal
    • If gaining 0.5 lbs/week: your actual TDEE = average intake - 250 kcal
  4. Adjust your calculator estimate to match your empirical TDEE.
  5. Recalculate every 10-15 lbs of body weight change.

See our TDEE accuracy guide for a deeper dive into validation methodology and expected error ranges.

Common Calculation Errors to Avoid

  • Forgetting to convert units: The formulas require kilograms and centimeters. Using pounds and inches will give wildly incorrect results.
  • Using the wrong sex constant: Men get +5, women get -161. Mixing these up changes BMR by 166 kcal.
  • Choosing too high an activity level: This is the most impactful error. See our tracking mistakes article for details.
  • Not recalculating after weight change: Every 10-15 lbs of body weight change shifts TDEE by 50-150 kcal.
  • Adding exercise calories on top of an already-active TDEE: If you used a 1.55 multiplier, exercise is already included. Adding more leads to double-counting.

Frequently Asked Questions

TDEE is calculated in two steps. First, calculate BMR using the Mifflin-St Jeor equation: For men: BMR = (10 × weight in kg) + (6.25 × height in cm) - (5 × age) + 5. For women: BMR = (10 × weight in kg) + (6.25 × height in cm) - (5 × age) - 161. Second, multiply BMR by an activity factor: Sedentary = 1.2, Lightly active = 1.375, Moderately active = 1.55, Very active = 1.725, Extra active = 1.9.

Use Katch-McArdle when you know your body fat percentage. The formula is: BMR = 370 + (21.6 × lean body mass in kg). It is more accurate than Mifflin-St Jeor for muscular individuals, those with obesity, and athletes because it accounts for body composition rather than using total body weight. If you do not know your body fat percentage, Mifflin-St Jeor is the better choice.

Choose based on your total weekly activity, not just gym sessions. Sedentary (1.2): desk job, no exercise. Lightly active (1.375): light exercise 1-3 days/week or a job requiring standing/walking. Manual calculation using Mifflin-St Jeor is accurate within approximately 10% for about 82% of the population, according to research by Frankenfield et al. (2005). The activity multiplier introduces additional variability. For best results, use the manual calculation as a starting point and validate it by tracking your food intake and body weight for 2-3 weeks.
