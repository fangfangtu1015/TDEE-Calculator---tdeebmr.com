What Is Basal Metabolic Rate (BMR)?

Basal Metabolic Rate (BMR) represents the minimum number of calories your body requires to sustain vital functions at complete rest. These functions include breathing, blood circulation, cell production, nutrient processing, protein synthesis, and maintaining body temperature. BMR accounts for approximately 60-75% of your Total Daily Energy Expenditure (TDEE), making it the single largest component of how many calories you burn each day.

BMR is measured under strict clinical conditions: after 8 hours of sleep, 12 hours of fasting, and in a thermoneutral environment (approximately 22-25 degrees Celsius). In everyday contexts, Resting Metabolic Rate (RMR) is often used interchangeably with BMR, though RMR is measured under less restrictive conditions and is typically 5-10% higher.

What Affects Your BMR?

Several physiological and demographic factors influence your basal metabolic rate:

  • Body size and composition: Larger bodies and those with more muscle mass have higher BMRs. Muscle tissue is metabolically more active than fat tissue, burning approximately 6-10 calories per pound per day at rest compared to 2-3 calories per pound for fat.
  • Age: BMR peaks in early adulthood and declines approximately 1-2% per decade after age 20, largely due to sarcopenia (age-related muscle loss) and hormonal changes.
  • Sex: Males typically have higher BMRs than females of the same age and weight because they tend to have more muscle mass and less body fat.
  • Genetics: Some individuals are born with naturally faster or slower metabolisms, though the variation attributable to genetics alone is estimated at about 2-8%.
  • Hormonal factors: Thyroid hormones (T3 and T4) are primary regulators of metabolic rate. Hyperthyroidism can increase BMR by 25-80%, while hypothyroidism can decrease it by 30-40%.
  • Environmental temperature: Extreme cold or heat forces the body to work harder to maintain core temperature, temporarily increasing BMR.

The Three BMR Formulas

Mifflin-St Jeor Equation (1990)

Developed by Mifflin, St Jeor, and colleagues and published in the American Journal of Clinical Nutrition, this equation is considered the most accurate predictive formula for resting energy expenditure in the general population. It was derived from a study of 498 subjects and has been validated in numerous subsequent studies.

Formula

Male: BMR = 10 x weight(kg) + 6.25 x height(cm) - 5 x age + 5
Female: BMR = 10 x weight(kg) + 6.25 x height(cm) - 5 x age - 161

The Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics recommends the Mifflin-St Jeor equation as the most reliable for estimating BMR in healthy individuals, with approximately 82% of predictions falling within 10% of measured values via indirect calorimetry.

Harris-Benedict Equation (1919, Revised 1984)

Originally published by James Harris and G Benedict in 1919 and revised by Roza and Shizgal in 1984, this was the gold standard for decades. The revised equation improved accuracy for contemporary populations but still tends to overestimate BMR by approximately 5% compared to modern measurements.

Revised Formula (Roza & Shizgal, 1984)

Male: BMR = 88.362 + 13.397 x weight(kg) + 4.799 x height(cm) - 5.677 x age
Female: BMR = 447.593 + 9.247 x weight(kg) + 3.098 x height(cm) - 4.330 x age

Katch-McArdle Formula (1996)

Unlike the other two equations, the Katch-McArdle formula uses lean body mass (LBM) rather than total body weight, making it more accurate for individuals with known body fat percentages. This is particularly valuable for athletes and muscular individuals whose higher muscle mass would cause other formulas to underestimate their BMR.

Formula

BMR = 370 + 21.6 x LBM(kg), where LBM = weight x (1 - body fat % / 100)

When accurate body fat data is available (from DEXA scan, hydrostatic weighing, or quality skinfold calipers), the Katch-McArdle formula achieves approximately 90% accuracy within 10% of measured values, outperforming both Mifflin-St Jeor and Harris-Benedict for this population.

How BMR Changes Over Time

Your BMR is not a fixed number. It changes in response to several factors:

Weight loss: When you lose weight, your BMR decreases because there is less body mass to maintain. This is partly why weight loss plateaus occur. A 10 kg weight loss can reduce BMR by approximately 100-150 calories per day. This reduction is sometimes greater than predicted by body composition changes alone, a phenomenon known as metabolic adaptation or adaptive thermogenesis.

Muscle gain: Building lean muscle mass increases BMR, though the effect is more modest than many fitness claims suggest. Each kilogram of muscle burns approximately 13 calories per day at rest. Gaining 2 kg of muscle would increase BMR by roughly 26 calories per day.

Aging: The age-related decline in BMR is largely mediated through muscle loss. Individuals who maintain regular resistance training throughout life can significantly attenuate this decline, preserving a higher BMR well into older age.

Dieting: Prolonged caloric restriction (especially below BMR) can trigger metabolic adaptation, reducing BMR beyond what would be expected from weight loss alone. Research by Leibel et al. (1995) demonstrated that prolonged semi-starvation can reduce BMR by up to 15% below predicted values. This is why diet breaks and refeed days are sometimes recommended during extended cutting phases.

Key Takeaway

BMR is your metabolic baseline. Use the Mifflin-St Jeor equation as your default, switch to Katch-McArdle if you know your body fat percentage, and remember that all formulas are estimates. Track your actual weight changes over 2-3 weeks and adjust your intake accordingly for the most reliable results.
