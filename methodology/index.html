Our Methodology

The scientific formulas, peer-reviewed research, and evidence-based standards behind every TDEEBMR calculator.

Home › Methodology
Last updated November 8, 2026

At TDEEBMR, transparency is foundational. Every number our calculators produce is derived from published, peer-reviewed scientific equations. This page documents each formula we use, the research supporting it, known limitations, and why we made the methodological choices we did. Our goal is to give you the same quality of estimation that a registered dietitian would use in a clinical setting, freely available in your browser.

Key Takeaway

TDEEBMR uses the Mifflin-St Jeor equation as its primary BMR formula because it has been validated as the most accurate predictive equation for resting energy expenditure in the general population. All activity multipliers are based on FAO/WHO/UNU Physical Activity Level (PAL) values.

Basal Metabolic Rate (BMR) Formulas

Basal Metabolic Rate represents the energy your body requires to sustain vital functions at complete rest: breathing, circulation, cellular production, nutrient processing, and protein turnover. BMR accounts for approximately 60 to 75 percent of total daily energy expenditure in most individuals. We implement three established BMR equations, each with distinct strengths.

Mifflin-St Jeor Equation (1990)

The Mifflin-St Jeor equation is our primary and default formula for estimating BMR. Published in 1990 by Mifflin, St Jeor, Hill, Scott, and Daugherty in the American Journal of Clinical Nutrition, this equation was developed from indirect calorimetry data collected on 498 healthy individuals (247 men, 251 women) aged 19 to 78.

The formula is:

Formula

Men: BMR = (10 × weight in kg) + (6.25 × height in cm) − (5 × age in years) + 5
Women: BMR = (10 × weight in kg) + (6.25 × height in cm) − (5 × age in years) − 161

In the original validation study, the Mifflin-St Jeor equation demonstrated significant improvements over the Harris-Benedict equation. A comprehensive review by Frankenfield, Roth-Yousey, Compher, and colleagues (2005), published in the Journal of the American Dietetic Association, analyzed multiple predictive equations against measured resting metabolic rate via indirect calorimetry. Their findings confirmed that the Mifflin-St Jeor equation produced estimates within ±10% of measured BMR for approximately 82% of individuals, making it the most accurate predictive equation available for the general, non-obese and obese population.

The Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics (formerly the American Dietetic Association) has endorsed the Mifflin-St Jeor equation as the preferred method for estimating resting energy expenditure in clinical practice. We use it as our default because it offers the best balance of accuracy, accessibility (it requires only weight, height, age, and sex), and validation across diverse populations.

Harris-Benedict Equation (1919, Revised 1984)

The Harris-Benedict equation is the oldest and most historically significant BMR formula. Originally published by James Arthur Harris and Francis Gano Benedict in 1919 through the Carnegie Institution of Washington, it was derived from data on 239 subjects. The equation was revised in 1984 by Annette M. Roza and Hashem M. Shizgal, who updated the coefficients using more modern population data.

The revised formula is:

Formula (Revised, 1984)

Men: BMR = (88.362 + 13.397 × weight in kg) + (4.799 × height in cm) − (5.677 × age in years)
Women: BMR = (447.593 + 9.247 × weight in kg) + (3.098 × height in cm) − (4.330 × age in years)

While historically important and still widely referenced, the Harris-Benedict equation is known to overestimate BMR by approximately 5% in modern populations. This overestimation occurs because the original data was collected from a relatively homogenous group of young, lean individuals in the early 20th century, and the revised coefficients still do not fully account for changes in body composition, lifestyle, and demographics seen in contemporary populations. The Frankenfield (2005) review found that only approximately 69% of individuals fell within ±10% of measured BMR when using the revised Harris-Benedict equation, compared to 82% for Mifflin-St Jeor.

We include the Harris-Benedict equation because many users encounter it in other contexts (fitness apps, older clinical literature) and want to compare results. We clearly label it as a secondary option and explain its tendency to overestimate.

Katch-McArdle Equation (1996)

The Katch-McArdle equation takes a fundamentally different approach: rather than using total body weight, it uses lean body mass (LBM) to estimate BMR. This makes it the most accurate formula for individuals who know their body fat percentage with reasonable precision, particularly athletes and muscular individuals whose higher muscle mass elevates BMR beyond what weight-based equations predict.

The formula is:

Formula

BMR = 370 + (21.6 × lean body mass in kg)
Where lean body mass = total body weight × (1 − body fat percentage / 100)

Published in William D. McArdle, Frank I. Katch, and Victor L. Katch's textbook Essentials of Exercise Physiology (1996), this equation is based on the well-established principle that metabolically active tissue (primarily muscle) is the main driver of resting energy expenditure. When body fat percentage is accurately measured (via DEXA scan, hydrostatic weighing, or quality skinfold calipers), the Katch-McArdle equation can achieve accuracy rates of approximately 90% within ±10% of measured BMR.

The trade-off is that it requires an additional input (body fat percentage) that most users do not know precisely. For this reason, we offer it as an option on our BMR calculator and link to our Body Fat Calculator to help users estimate their body fat percentage first.

Activity Level Multipliers (PAL Values)

To convert BMR into Total Daily Energy Expenditure, we multiply by a Physical Activity Level (PAL) factor. Our multipliers are derived from the FAO/WHO/UNU expert consultation on energy requirements, published in 2001 as part of the report "Human Energy Requirements."

Activity LevelPAL MultiplierDescriptionExamples
Sedentary1.2Little or no exerciseDesk job, less than 4,000 steps/day
Lightly Active1.375Light exercise 1-3 days/weekDaily walks, light yoga, some household activity
Moderately Active1.55Moderate exercise 3-5 days/weekRegular gym sessions, active commute, 8,000-12,000 steps/day
Very Active1.725Hard exercise 6-7 days/weekIntense daily training, physically demanding job
Extra Active1.9Very hard exercise, physical jobProfessional athletes, military training, heavy labor

The PAL system is the internationally accepted standard for expressing habitual physical activity levels. A PAL of 1.2 represents the minimum energy expenditure needed to sustain a sedentary lifestyle, while a PAL of 1.9 represents the upper range of sustainable physical activity for most individuals. The FAO/WHO/UNU consultation specifically recommended these values for use in estimating total energy requirements in population groups and individuals.

We use these standardized values rather than proprietary multipliers because they are transparent, reproducible, and grounded in metabolic research. When in doubt, we recommend users select the lower activity level, as research consistently shows that most people overestimate their physical activity.

Body Fat Estimation: U.S. Navy Method

Our Body Fat Calculator uses the U.S. Navy circumference method, originally developed by Hodgdon and Beckett in 1984 at the Naval Health Research Center in San Diego, California.

The method estimates body fat percentage using body circumference measurements:

  • For men: neck circumference, abdominal circumference (at navel), and height
  • For women: neck circumference, natural waist circumference (narrowest point), hip circumference (widest point), and height

The original validation study, published as a Naval Health Research Center report, demonstrated that the method correlated well with hydrostatic weighing (the gold standard at the time) with standard errors of estimate around 3-4% for men and 3-5% for women. The method was subsequently adopted by the U.S. Department of Defense for body composition assessment of military personnel.

While not as precise as DEXA scans or air displacement plethysmography (Bod Pod), the Navy method offers a practical, accessible estimation that requires only a measuring tape. We recommend users take measurements first thing in the morning, before eating, and use a flexible tape measure held snugly but not tight against the skin for best results.

Body Mass Index (BMI) Classification

Our BMI calculator follows the World Health Organization's standard classification system for body mass index categories. BMI is calculated as weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared (kg/m²).

These thresholds are defined by the WHO in its global database and technical reports on obesity. We note that BMI is a population-level screening tool and does not distinguish between muscle mass and fat mass. Athletes and very muscular individuals may classify as "overweight" or even "obese" by BMI despite having healthy body composition.

Ideal Weight Formulas

Our Ideal Weight Calculator implements four established clinical formulas, each developed from different population data and historical contexts:

Devine Formula (1974)

Originally developed by Dr. Benjamin Devine for dosing of medications that distribute primarily in lean tissue. Published in a clinical pharmacy context, it remains the most widely used ideal body weight formula in medical settings.

Men: 50.0 kg + 2.3 kg per inch over 5 feet
Women: 45.5 kg + 2.3 kg per inch over 5 feet

Robinson Formula (1983)

Developed by Dr. J.D. Robinson and colleagues as an update to the Devine formula, using more contemporary population data from military health surveys.

Men: 52.0 kg + 1.9 kg per inch over 5 feet
Women: 49.0 kg + 1.7 kg per inch over 5 feet

Miller Formula (1983)

Published by Dr. D.R. Miller as an alternative estimation that tends to produce slightly higher values, particularly for taller individuals.

Men: 56.2 kg + 1.41 kg per inch over 5 feet
Women: 53.1 kg + 1.36 kg per inch over 5 feet

Hamwi Formula (1964)

One of the earliest ideal body weight formulas, developed by Dr. George Hamwi for clinical use in diabetes management and nutritional assessment.

Men: 48.0 kg + 2.7 kg per inch over 5 feet
Women: 45.5 kg + 2.2 kg per inch over 5 feet

We present all four formulas side by side because they can vary by 5-10 kg depending on height, and there is no single universally accepted "ideal" weight. Clinical context matters: the Devine formula is preferred for drug dosing, while the others may be more appropriate for general health assessment.

Protein Intake Recommendations

Our Macros Calculator bases its protein recommendations on the International Society of Sports Nutrition (ISSN) position stand on protein and exercise, as well as the Recommended Dietary Allowance (RDA) established by the Institute of Medicine.

The RDA for protein is 0.8 grams per kilogram of body weight per day for sedentary adults. However, the ISSN position stand (Jager et al., 2017) and subsequent research recommend significantly higher intakes for active individuals:

  • Sedentary adults (RDA): 0.8 g/kg/day
  • Recreational exercisers: 1.2-1.4 g/kg/day
  • Strength training / muscle building: 1.6-2.2 g/kg/day
  • Athletes in caloric deficit: 1.8-2.7 g/kg/day (higher to preserve lean mass)

We use 1.6-2.2 g/kg as the default range for our active users because this range has been shown in meta-analyses (Morton et al., 2018) to maximize muscle protein synthesis and lean mass gains from resistance training. For users in a caloric deficit, we recommend the upper end of this range to minimize muscle loss.

Water Intake Estimation

Our Water Intake Calculator uses the body weight-based formula commonly cited in clinical nutrition literature: 30-35 milliliters per kilogram of body weight per day under normal conditions.

This range is consistent with the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) adequate intake guidelines and the U.S. Institute of Medicine's general recommendations (approximately 2.7 L/day for women and 3.7 L/day for men from all sources, including food moisture). We adjust upward based on activity level and environmental factors, adding approximately 350-700 mL for each 30 minutes of moderate exercise, consistent with American College of Sports Medicine (ACSM) guidelines.

Calorie Deficit and the 7,700 kcal Rule

Our Calorie Deficit Calculator uses the widely accepted approximation that one kilogram of body fat stores approximately 7,700 kilocalories of energy. This value is derived from the energy density of adipose tissue (approximately 87% lipid) multiplied by the caloric content of human fat (9.4 kcal/g).

To calculate the daily deficit needed to reach a target weight within a specified timeframe, we divide the total caloric deficit (target weight loss in kg × 7,700) by the number of days in the timeline. We cap recommended daily deficits at 1,000 kcal/day (approximately 0.9 kg/week) because larger deficits increase the risk of muscle loss, metabolic adaptation, and nutrient deficiencies, consistent with guidelines from the American College of Sports Medicine and the Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics.

Limitations and Why These Are Estimates

Important: Understanding the Limitations All formulas used by TDEEBMR provide estimates, not exact measurements. Individual metabolic rates can vary by 10-15% or more from predicted values due to genetic factors, hormonal status, organ mass variation, gut microbiome composition, and other biological variables not captured by population-based equations.

Several key limitations should be understood:

  • Population averages: All predictive equations were developed from population data. Your individual metabolism may differ significantly from the average person of your age, sex, weight, and height.
  • Measurement error: Body fat estimation methods (including the Navy method) have inherent error margins of 3-5%. Errors in input measurements compound through the calculations.
  • Metabolic adaptation: During prolonged caloric restriction, the body adapts by reducing energy expenditure beyond what would be predicted from weight loss alone. This phenomenon, documented by Fothergill et al. (2016) in the "BIGGEST LOSER" study, means that actual caloric needs during dieting may be lower than calculated values.
  • Activity variability: Daily energy expenditure varies significantly from day to day. The PAL multipliers represent habitual activity levels, not daily fluctuations.
  • Body composition changes: As you lose or gain weight, your BMR changes. Calculations should be updated every 4-6 weeks during active dieting or bulking phases.

We recommend using our calculator results as starting points and adjusting based on actual outcomes. Track your weight daily under consistent conditions (morning, fasted, after bathroom use) and compare weekly averages to your predicted trajectory. If results diverge by more than 0.5 kg per week from predictions, adjust your caloric intake by 100-200 kcal and recalculate.
